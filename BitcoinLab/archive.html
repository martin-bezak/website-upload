Most ideas failed. That is the useful part: each failure narrowed the search. This is every step from the first prototype to the three strategies, with the numbers as they were measured.

  • Failed did not beat a simple benchmark
  • Partial useful, but not proven or not enough
  • Works passed every test
  • Method a change in how we test
  • Retired built and shown for a while, then removed
  • Live running now, recorded every night

  1. 01

    Market Rhymes: has this chart happened before? Failed

    Idea. Take the last 26 weeks of a market, find the 10 most similar stretches in 50 years of 15 markets (indices, stocks, gold, oil, currencies), and see what came next.

    Test. The same search on 1,586 past dates, using only data available at the time.

    Result. The matches got the 3-month direction right 57% of the time. Simply saying “up” every time was right 64%. Famous example: before Lehman (Sep 2008) the matches said +5%; the S&P 500 fell 30%.

    Lesson: similar-looking charts add noise, not information. Any signal has to beat the laziest rule first.

  2. 02

    Market Rhymes, crypto: 200 coins, live Failed

    Idea. The same matching on daily data from 584 coins on Binance (delisted ones included), updated every night.

    Result. 22,693 past calls: right 52% of the time on the 30-day direction. “Always down” was right 59%, because most coins lose value. Coins moved about the same after “up” calls as after “down” calls.

    Lesson: the benchmark depends on the market. In crypto the lazy rule is “down”, and pattern matching lost to it too. Also learned: reused tickers (LUNA, VEN) create fake price jumps and must be split.

  3. 03

    Bitcoin Lab forecasts: seven models, 1 hour to 1 year Retired

    Idea. Focus on Bitcoin with much richer data (hourly prices, funding rates, open interest, long/short ratios) and let seven models forecast the chance of a rise: base rate, momentum, similar charts, a price model, a price + derivatives model, a volatility model, and their average.

    Test. Refit every month on data available then, scored with Brier skill, calibration and a Diebold–Mariano test against the base rate.

    Result. At 1 and 4 hours the models were slightly but significantly better (53% right vs 51%), but trading on it lost everything to fees. From 7 to 90 days nothing beat the base rate. At 1 year there were only about 6 independent outcomes, too few to judge. Similar charts were worse than the base rate (48% vs 53% at 7 days, p < 0.001) and were removed.

    Lesson: direction is close to unpredictable. But the volatility model’s ranges were well calibrated: how big moves will be is predictable even when which way isn’t. That idea led to the squeeze signal below. The forecast page itself was retired: probabilities that don’t beat the base rate aren’t worth showing next to the strategies.

  4. 04

    Strategy 1: trend + volatility target + trailing stop Partial

    Idea. Hold Bitcoin when it is above its 50-, 100- and 200-day averages, sized for 50% volatility, with a trailing stop 3× the average daily range below the high.

    Result. Apr 2018–Oct 2026: $1 became 7.2× against Bitcoin’s 10.9×, but the worst drop was −36% instead of −77% and it sat out 2018 and 2022 entirely. Only 35% of trades won, but winners averaged +26.5% and losers −3.9%. Beating buy-and-hold on risk-adjusted terms was not proven (p = 0.23).

    Lesson: a crash shield, not a money machine. Later tests showed the trailing stop was the problem: the same trend rule without it turned $1 into 24.6×.

  5. 05

    Strategy 2: crowded leverage reversal Failed

    Idea. When funding, futures premium, open interest and top traders’ positions all show crowded longs (top 15% of the past year), bet on a flush; the mirror for crowded shorts.

    Result. Only 9–20 trades since 2021. Selling into crowded longs lost on 7 of 9 trades. Each of the four parts tested on its own also failed (best: top traders’ positioning, p = 0.07 before correction).

    Lesson: in bull markets, crowded longs mean “trend”, not “top”. Real liquidation data from several exchanges might do better; Binance-only daily averages don’t.

  6. 06

    Strategy 3: volatility squeeze breakout Works sell side

    Idea. Use the part that is predictable. After an unusually calm stretch, a break out of the 20-day range starts a big move. Stop on the other side of the range, target twice the risk, at most 20 days.

    Result. Holding Bitcoin and selling only on breakdowns turned $1 into 60.9× against 12.7× (Sep 2018–Oct 2026). Breakdowns beat random sell times with identical exits in 995 of 1,000 simulations. All 27 nearby settings beat buy-and-hold. Without its single best trade it still made 38.9×. Buying breakouts was weaker evidence (p = 0.07).

    Lesson: the first real signal: calm, then break down, then get out.

  7. 07

    The audit: were we thorough enough? Method

    What changed. Every strategy went through one shared hour-by-hour engine and the same checks: both halves of history, every year, costs ×2 and ×3, a timing placebo, block bootstrap, and a multiple-testing correction across five hypotheses registered before running.

    Result. Only the squeeze breakdown survived the correction (p = 0.010). It also worked on a daily-close trigger (61× against 13×), which a nightly website can deliver. Similar charts as a trade strategy lost 55%; crowded leverage failed in every form.

    Lesson: test the version you can actually deliver, and count every test you run.

  8. 08

    The combination: trend + squeezes Works

    Idea. Trend as the base position, squeeze breakdowns and breakouts as overrides. A volatility-sized version for lower risk.

    Result. Sep 2018–Oct 2026: $1 became 28× (Combo A) and 18× (Combo B) against Bitcoin’s 13×, with worst drops of −43% and −28% instead of −77%. All 27 nearby settings beat buy-and-hold on return, Sharpe and drawdown. From six different start dates, they beat Bitcoin’s Sharpe every time, and its return every time but mid-2023.

    Lesson: the trend filter does most of the work; squeeze breakouts add a little; the breakdown sell matters most when you hold Bitcoin all the time.

  9. 09

    Improvements, and the overfitting trap Method

    Idea. Test four tweaks (smoother trend, a 2% buffer, averaging three sets of averages, volatility sizing) and interest on cash, judged by walk-forward selection: each year, pick the best of 32 versions using only past data.

    Result. Picking the “best” version each year did worse than the fixed rules ($8.2 against $16.4 per $1 from 2020), so no tuning. Interest on cash added about 2% a year at no extra risk and was adopted. The smoother trend was worse in 13 of 16 comparisons and was rejected.

    Lesson: the settings that looked best in the past didn’t stay best. Fixed, simple rules beat clever tuning here.

  10. 10

    Trading later than the backtest Method

    Problem. Bitcoin trades around the clock, but the rules read daily closes, and the backtest traded right at the 00:00 UTC close. This site can only update around 03:30 UTC, once the day’s data is published, so anyone following it trades hours later.

    Test. Every trade re-run 1 to 12 hours after the close, with stops and targets unchanged.

    Result. Returns barely moved (Growth 44× to 58×, Balanced 31× to 34×, Defensive 20× to 22×, against Bitcoin’s 12×). Worst drops moved more: Balanced −44% to −49%, Defensive −28% to −34%, built up over many small differences in the 2019–20 sideways market, not one bad trade.

    Kept going. The test now also runs every night: the Strategy page keeps a small score of each strategy traded 4 hours late, next to the real one, so the live record shows whether the delay matters.

    Lesson: the edge doesn’t depend on catching the exact close, but a single worst-drop number overstates how precisely it is known.

  11. 11

    Today: three strategies Live

    Growth (squeeze sell + interest), Balanced (Combo A + interest) and Defensive (Combo B + interest). Their live record starts in October 2026 and has its own chart: $100,000 from the first live night, with nothing from the backtest mixed in. That record, not the backtest, will decide whether they really work. See today’s actions · Read the rules.

    Still open: stock and ETF data for a public site needs a licence; buy and sell signals may need a legal check before wider sharing; ETH could be a second asset.
